💼 Non-Coding Tech Roles

Real careers, real salaries, real growth. Not a consolation prize.#

🏠 Home • 💼 All tracks • 🧪 QA/SDET • 🤖 Data/AI

Read this if you're struggling with code#

Not everyone in tech writes code. That is completely normal and always has been.

Behind every engineer at Google there's a product manager deciding what to build, a business analyst mapping requirements, a support engineer keeping customers running, and a technical writer explaining it all. These people are paid well, promoted, and respected.

If you've given coding an honest 6+ months — real daily effort, not two frustrated weeks — and it still isn't clicking, that's information, not failure. Some of the most successful people in tech are the ones who figured out early that their strength was communication, structure or product sense rather than algorithms.

Important

Give coding a fair trial first. Six months of genuine daily practice. Most students who say "coding is not for me" quit in week 3, during the phase where it's hard for everyone. If you haven't done six honest months, do those first. → core/dsa.md

Also true: these roles pay less than senior engineering at the start — but the ceiling is high. Senior product managers and engineering managers out-earn most senior developers.

📊 Business Analyst#

What you do: sit between business stakeholders and engineers. Gather requirements, write specs and user stories, map processes, define acceptance criteria, run UAT.

Learn:

  • SQL ⭐ — the single most valuable skill here. Joins, aggregations, subqueries
  • Excel — pivot tables, VLOOKUP/XLOOKUP, charts, basic modelling
  • Requirements documentation — BRD, FRD, user stories, acceptance criteria
  • Process mapping — flowcharts, BPMN, draw.io
  • Agile & Scrum — sprints, backlog grooming, the BA's role
  • JIRA + Confluence
  • Power BI or Tableau — dashboards
  • Wireframing — Figma basics
  • Basic understanding of APIs and databases

Certifications that help: ECBA (IIBA), Scrum Master (CSM/PSM I — PSM I is ~$200 and widely respected)

Who hires: Deloitte, EY, PwC, KPMG, Accenture, Capgemini, TCS, Infosys, banks, insurance, every mid-to-large product company

🎧 Technical Support Engineer#

What you do: solve customer technical problems — debug configurations, read logs, reproduce issues, escalate real bugs to engineering, write knowledge-base articles.

Why it's underrated: it's the easiest entry into a good product company, and support engineers move into QA, DevOps, product and solutions roles constantly. You learn the product deeply and you talk to real users daily — which is exactly what makes you valuable later.

Learn:

  • Troubleshooting method — reproduce → isolate → diagnose → resolve → document
  • Linux basics — logs, processes, permissions, grep, tail
  • Networking — DNS, HTTP status codes, ports, firewalls, TCP/IP
  • SQL — query customer data
  • APIs — Postman, reading API docs, interpreting error responses
  • Ticketing tools — Zendesk, Freshdesk, ServiceNow, JIRA
  • Written and spoken communication ⭐ — this is 50% of the job
  • Reading logs and stack traces

Who hires: Freshworks, Zoho, Salesforce, Microsoft, Adobe, Atlassian, SAP, ServiceNow, most SaaS companies

☁️ Cloud Support Engineer#

What you do: support customers running on AWS/Azure/GCP — debug their infrastructure, networking, permissions and performance issues.

Why it's excellent: these teams actively hire freshers, pay well, and are a direct pipeline into DevOps, SRE and solutions architecture. AWS Premium Support, Microsoft Azure Support and Oracle Cloud all run large India-based fresher hiring programmes.

Learn:

  • One cloud, properly — AWS recommended
  • Core services — EC2, S3, VPC, IAM, RDS, Lambda, CloudWatch
  • Linux — solidly → core/git-and-linux.md
  • Networking — VPC, subnets, routing, DNS, load balancers, security groups
  • Troubleshooting methodology
  • Basic scripting — Python or Bash
  • AWS Cloud Practitioner → Solutions Architect Associate (these genuinely get resumes past filters)

Who hires: AWS, Microsoft, Oracle, Google Cloud, Rackspace, plus every cloud MSP

📖 Related: devops-cloud.md

📈 Data Analyst#

What you do: answer business questions with data. Write SQL, build dashboards, analyse trends, present findings to stakeholders.

Learn:

  • SQL ⭐⭐ — the #1 skill. Joins, GROUP BY, window functions, CTEs
  • Excel — advanced
  • Power BI or Tableau ⭐ — Power BI is more common in Indian companies
  • Python — pandas, matplotlib (optional but a big advantage)
  • Statistics — averages, distributions, correlation, A/B testing basics
  • Data cleaning and validation
  • Business metrics — retention, churn, funnels, cohorts, unit economics
  • Storytelling with data — the presentation matters as much as the analysis

Who hires: essentially every company. Consulting firms, e-commerce, fintech, banks, startups.

📖 Deeper roadmap: data-ai-ml.md

🎯 Product Analyst / Associate PM#

What you do: decide what gets built. Talk to users, analyse product data, write specs, prioritise the roadmap, work with engineering and design.

Honest note: APM roles are highly competitive and often favour tier-1 graduates. But Product Analyst roles are more accessible and lead to PM within 2–3 years.

Learn:

  • SQL ⭐ — non-negotiable for product analytics
  • Product analytics tools — Mixpanel, Amplitude, Google Analytics
  • Metrics thinking — North Star metric, AARRR funnel, retention, cohorts
  • A/B testing — design, statistical significance, interpretation
  • User research — interviews, surveys, usability testing
  • Writing PRDs and user stories
  • Figma basics, wireframing
  • Product sense — read Lenny's Newsletter, Inspired by Marty Cagan, Hooked by Nir Eyal

How to break in as a tier-3 student: build and launch something real (an app, a tool, a community), write publicly about product decisions, do product teardowns on LinkedIn, apply to startups where the bar is skill rather than pedigree.

Who hires: startups, e-commerce, fintech, SaaS

✍️ Technical Writer#

What you do: write documentation, API references, user guides, tutorials and release notes.

Why it suits some people well: if you write clearly and understand technology conceptually, this is a comfortable, well-paid, low-stress career with strong remote opportunities.

Learn:

  • Excellent written English ⭐ — the whole job
  • Markdown and docs-as-code
  • Git and GitHub (docs live in repos)
  • Documentation tools — Docusaurus, MkDocs, GitBook, Confluence
  • API documentation — OpenAPI/Swagger, Postman
  • Basic coding literacy — enough to read code and run examples
  • Screenshots, diagrams, screen recordings
  • Information architecture — structuring docs so people find things

Portfolio: contribute documentation to open-source projects. This is the fastest way in — maintainers desperately need docs help, and it's public, verifiable proof.

Who hires: every product company. Strong remote and freelance market.

🔧 Implementation / Solutions Consultant#

What you do: configure and deploy the company's product for enterprise clients — setup, integrations, data migration, training, go-live support.

Learn:

  • SQL and data migration
  • APIs and integrations
  • The product domain (CRM, ERP, HRMS, etc.)
  • Project management basics
  • Client communication ⭐
  • Excel, documentation
  • Sometimes a low-code platform (Salesforce, ServiceNow, SAP)

Who hires: Salesforce partners, ServiceNow partners, SAP, Zoho, Freshworks, enterprise SaaS. Salesforce and ServiceNow ecosystems have huge fresher demand in India and free official learning platforms (Trailhead, ServiceNow Learning).

💰 Sales Engineer / Pre-Sales#

What you do: the technical half of a sales team — run demos, design solutions, answer technical objections, write proposals and RFP responses.

Why it pays well: base + commission. Strong pre-sales engineers earn more than many developers.

Learn:

  • Deep product knowledge
  • Presentation and demo skills ⭐
  • Solution architecture basics
  • Cloud fundamentals
  • Objection handling, business communication
  • Proposal and RFP writing

Who hires: every B2B SaaS company, cloud providers, enterprise vendors

Important

Learn SQL. It is the common requirement across every role on this page, and it's genuinely learnable in 4–6 weeks. It's the highest-return skill for non-coding tech careers. → LeetCode SQL 50

❓ "Why not software development?" — how to answer

You will be asked this. Never answer with "I'm not good at coding."

Bad: "I tried coding but couldn't do DSA, so I chose this."

Good: "I spent a year learning development and I can read code and work with APIs and databases comfortably. What I found is that I'm strongest at understanding what users actually need and translating that into clear requirements — I enjoyed the requirements and testing side of my projects more than the implementation. That's why I'm targeting BA roles, and the technical background means I can talk to engineers properly."

Why this works: it's honest, it frames your technical background as an asset rather than a failed attempt, and it shows self-awareness — which is exactly the trait these roles select for.

⚠️ Mistakes in this path#

MistakeFix
Choosing this after 3 weeks of coding frustrationGive coding 6 honest months first. Weeks 3–12 are hard for everyone.
Skipping SQLSQL is required for nearly all of these roles. Learn it properly.
Treating it as "the easy option"These roles have their own high bars — communication, structure, domain depth.
Weak communication skillsThis is the core skill here. Practise writing and speaking deliberately.
No portfolioBuild one: sample BRDs, dashboards, documentation contributions, product teardowns.
Apologising for the choice in interviewsOwn it. Confidence in your reasoning is what's being assessed.
Ignoring certificationsUnlike dev roles, certs genuinely matter here — Scrum, AWS, Salesforce, Power BI.

Tech is much bigger than writing code. These are careers, not fallbacks.#
